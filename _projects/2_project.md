Despite its ubiquity among bilinguals, code-switching (CS), or the alternation between languages in conversation, has historically increased processing costs in time-based psycholinguistic studies of bilingual comprehension. This context-cognition paradox may result from a mismatch between experimental task and CS ecology: when CS experimental paradigms approximate its naturalistic use, habitual code-switchers demonstrate processing cost reductions commensurate with a switched structure’s distributional frequency. In Spanish-English, this is evidenced by the processing of bilingual compound verbs (BCVs). Perfect BCVs comprising the auxiliary *haber* ‘have’ and an English past participle seldom appear within the verbal complex (i.e., *haber* + V<sub>Perf</sub>; *el científico ha* tested … ‘the scientist has …’), whereas progressive switches between the Spanish auxiliary *estar* ‘be’ and an English present participle (i.e., *estar* + V<sub>Prog</sub>; *el científico está testing* … ‘the scientist is …’) are comparatively frequent. Unattested switch types (i.e., *haber* + V<sub>Inf</sub>) are found to yield greater processing costs. However, these findings alone are not sufficient to adjudicate between syntactically- and experientially-driven accounts of CS cost attenuation given the cross-community evidence of this asymmetry, nor do they address the impact of interindividual variability in CS engagement. The present study examines whether bilingual groups with distinct CS exposure profiles (i.e., late Spanish L2 speakers [Experiments 1 & 2] and Spanish heritage speakers [Experiment 3] demonstrate online sensitivity to grammatically permissible but regionally unattested light verb BCVs (i.e., *hacer* + V<sub>Inf</sub>; *el científico hace* test … ‘the scientist tests …’). Implications for experience-based models of language processing, task-ecology misalignment, and traditional understandings of switch costs are discussed.
